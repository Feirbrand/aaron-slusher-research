## Canonical definition

*A repeatable process for interrupting a cascade, regaining footing, and clearing and preparing the pathway back to capability that remains present but obstructed.*

**Its question:** How do we stop being carried by the cascade, regain footing, remove or bypass the obstruction, and prepare the route?
**Its output:** A cleared, ready pathway.

Capability that is out of reach is not capability that is gone — the capability is present but obstructed. Parate is the work of clearing and preparing the pathway back to it.

## The sequence

**Out of Reach → Pathway → Unlock → Execute**

## What's in the Name

> In Spanish, *párate* means stop — stop the cascade before it takes over.
>
> In Latin, *parate* means get ready — prepare, clear the pathway back to what you already know.
>
> Put together: **the moment you stop the cascade is the same moment you get ready to clear the pathway back.**

*Parate* is Latin — the imperative of *parare*, "to prepare, make ready." Its Spanish descendant *párate* means "stop" and "stand up." Same root, two documented branches, and each one names a real moment in the process — sequential, not simultaneous:

1. **Párate** (Spanish) — interrupt the cascade. Regain footing. Stop letting pressure drive.
2. **Parate** (Latin) — clear the obstruction. Prepare the pathway back to what is still there.

Control has to be interrupted before the way can be prepared. That order doesn't reverse. Not wordplay — this is what the word has always meant: centuries ago, "get ready" in Latin narrowed to "stop / stand still" in Spanish, while Latin kept the original sense. Saying "Parate" says both at once.

It appears in the Latin Vulgate at Isaiah 40:3, Matthew 3:3, and Mark 1:3 — *"Parate viam Domini,"* prepare the way of the Lord — said of someone whose whole job was clearing the path so someone else could arrive. Same posture the process is built on. The word was chosen, not coined.
