# Ground. Ascent. Altitude.™

**Status:** In active use. White paper in preparation.

**Named and elevated:** July 28, 2026

## What it is

Ground. Ascent. Altitude.™ (GAA) is the three-stage developmental structure that the [Elevation Grid™](../elevation-grid/)'s nine positions are organized under.

The Grid and GAA are the same architecture at two resolutions. The Grid gives you a coordinate — where a person or system is operating right now. GAA gives you the row that coordinate sits in, and what that row's job is.

## The three stages

**Ground** — Row 1. Restore enough stability to function.

**Ascent** — Row 2. Rebuild and strengthen the route.

**Altitude** — Row 3. Integrate it so it holds under real, changing load.

## One system, one name

GAA is a single shared name across every domain that applies it — the same way OODA Loop or PDCA work. One name, explained in each field that uses it, never renamed per industry.

The stage names do not change per brand. Position names within the Grid are domain-specific; the three stages are not. Three competing sets of stage names would undo the compression GAA exists to create.

**The governing rule:** *Architecture fixed. Vocabulary flexes.*

## The recursive rule

Dropping from Altitude back toward Ground under new load — a serious injury, a major disruption, a system failure — is not failure. It is the architecture working as designed.

## Where it sits

> The Parate Process™ clears and prepares the pathway.
> Ground. Ascent. Altitude.™ builds what must hold.

GAA runs under any application built on this architecture. It is not the property of a single product or cohort.

Like every NFA component, it operates inside adaptive cognition infrastructure (ACI) — the same domain the whole architecture sits inside.

## Origin

The structure was in use before it had this name. It appears in print in February 2026, published in the Elevation Grid™ paper as the row organization under that framework's nine positions.

July 28, 2026 was the date it was named and elevated to a first-class component — not the date it was created. The pattern was recognized, not invented.
